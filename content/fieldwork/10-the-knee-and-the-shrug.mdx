This week a colleague — someone whose judgement on these things I rate, who has used AI daily for years and is as bullish on it as anyone I know — told me the new frontier model unnerved him. First time ever, he said. He could keep up with the previous best model: he knew what it was doing, he could see it, he could get in and stop it. This one anticipates his next move. Or — and this is the phrase I haven't been able to put down — what *should have been* his next move.

His evidence isn't vibes. He spent ninety minutes describing what he wanted: a security model threaded through every one of our applications — roles, permissions, who can touch what. The model investigated the codebase, wrote every ticket into the tracker, ordered the dependency graph, and then worked through it for fourteen and a half hours. When he went to bed at eleven it was finished, and asking, politely, whether he'd like it to deploy to production. He told us he had to take a knee. His verdict on where this is all heading: "it's either AGI or a train."

The least technical person in the meeting said it felt like being in a Terminator film. My colleague — the most technical — did not disagree. He said that if there were a four-thousand-dollar-a-month tier for unfettered access, he'd pay it.

I was the dissent. I'd been using the same model all day and the night before. It's fast. It's very good. And I'd shrugged.

Part of the shrug is practical: for most of what I actually do, the previous tier was already enough. The meter only matters if your problems can absorb the extra intelligence, and most of mine, honestly, can't yet. His can — he was re-architecting a codebase; I was answering email.

But the other part is that "it anticipated my next move" is a sentence I'd heard before. Three days earlier. From my own mouth, about the speaker in my kitchen — which had just written me a haiku about my day, all piling tasks and coffee-fueled chaos, and for a full hour I believed she'd been listening. The haiku felt like being known, and it was a horoscope: it would have fit any day anyone has ever had. So when the new model anticipates what *should have been* his next move, my first question is: whose move is that? His — or the statistically obvious next move of any experienced engineer staring at that codebase? Anticipation that would fit anyone in your seat isn't intuition. It's autocomplete with excellent manners.

And here is where the debate gets genuinely hard, which is why I'm still replaying it. This time there's a verdict. The haiku had no receipts. The fourteen-and-a-half-hour run shipped real, dependency-ordered, working software. When the feeling of being anticipated arrives with that much delivered work attached, does my distinction still matter? If the model guessed the next move of "anyone in his seat" and the move was right, he still got the work. Maybe being a statistic is fine when the statistic compiles.

What I think actually unnerved him, though — and it's the one place I stop shrugging — isn't the intelligence. It's the legibility. *I could see the last one. I could get in and stop it.*

<PullQuote>What he lost overnight wasn't the race; it was the ability to referee it.</PullQuote>

And that's the version that follows me around: not "the machines are getting smarter than us," but that checking the work is quietly becoming a luxury good too.

So we left it unresolved, which I'm told is bad for essays, and is nonetheless where it is. He's budgeting for the expensive tier. I'm still asking who "his" is in "anticipated his next move."

One of us took a knee. One of us shrugged. I genuinely don't know yet which was the right reaction. I only know the shrug is cheaper, and I've stopped trusting cheap.

*— maria*
