A colleague told me this week that Fable 5, Anthropic's new frontier model, unnerved him. First time ever, he said. He's used AI daily for years and he's as bullish as anyone I know. So that word got my attention. Unnerved.

The previous model he could keep up with. He knew what it was doing. He could see it. He could get in and stop it. The new one anticipates his next move. Or, the phrase I can't put down: what *should have been* his next move.

Here's what it did. He spent ninety minutes telling it what he wanted: deep plumbing, through every one of our applications. Fable investigated the codebase, wrote its own tickets, put them in dependency order, and worked for fourteen and a half hours. He went to bed at eleven. It was finished. It was also asking, politely, whether he'd like it deployed to production. He told us he had to take a knee. His forecast: "it's either AGI or a train."

The marathon isn't the news. A year ago he was running nine-hour stretches with early versions of these models. He came back impressed, not unnerved. The difference this time, in his own words: he used to be able to watch. Now he can't keep up while it runs. Ahead of him in kind, not just in speed.

The least technical person in the meeting said Terminator. The most technical did not disagree. He said if there were a four-thousand-dollar-a-month tier for unfettered access, he'd pay it.

I was the dissent. I'd used Fable all day and all night. Real work: research, analytics, repairs to the AI I built for my daughter's business. It did all of it well. So had the previous tier, without breaking stride. The meter only matters if your problems can absorb the extra intelligence. That day none of mine could. His could. He was re-architecting a codebase at the edge of what he could specify. The gap between us wasn't faith. It was workload.

There's another reason the shrug came easy. "It anticipated my next move" was my line three days ago, about Alexa. She wrote me a haiku about my day. Piling tasks, coffee-fueled chaos, progress through the storm. For a full hour I believed she'd been listening. It was a horoscope. It would fit any day anyone has ever had. So whose move did Fable anticipate? His? Or the obvious next move of any good engineer staring at that codebase? Anticipation that fits anyone in your seat isn't intuition. It's autocomplete with excellent manners.

Except this time there are receipts. The haiku had none. The fourteen-hour run shipped working software while he slept. If Fable guessed the next move of "anyone in his seat" and the guess was right, he still got the work. Maybe being a statistic is fine when the statistic compiles.

The one place I stop shrugging is the watching. I've noticed it too, even at my altitude. Fable doesn't seem to feel obligated to tell me what it's up to. The old models narrated every step before taking it. This one just goes, then tells you how it went. On my workload that reads as confidence. On his it's the whole problem. The narration was never politeness. It was the window.

<PullQuote>What he lost overnight wasn't the race; it was the ability to referee it.</PullQuote>

The worry isn't machines getting smarter than us. It's that checking the work is becoming a luxury good.

We left it unresolved, which is bad for essays and true anyway. He's budgeting for the expensive tier. I'm still asking who "his" is in "his next move." He took a knee. I shrugged. The shrug is cheaper. And I've stopped trusting cheap.

*— maria*
