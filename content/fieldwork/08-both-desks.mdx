Two jobs.

One is the new business. Startup, CEO chair, everything I touch is mine. I run the experiments. I pick the tools. I am building the thing.

The other is the part-time corp job I've held for years and intend to keep. Different shape entirely. Many people, real budgets, a desk that has been a desk for a long time. The tools are decided several rungs above me by people who have to think about data-leakage and audit trails and the consequences of a model walking out the back door with someone else's quarterly numbers. They are not wrong to think about it.

Today the second job needed a one-pager. The kind business people print and put on the wall. *Here is the system we're moving from. Here is the system we're moving to. Here is what your dashboard will look like, eventually. Here are the three things you need to do this week.* Strategy, in three colours and a bordered box.

Years ago I would have done that on a quiet Tuesday. Today it took me most of a day. Some of that was real research — the new tool genuinely behaves differently. Most of it was the slow work of dragging boxes around in PowerPoint by hand, with nothing helping me at the boundary between brain and slide.

I tried, of course. I asked Copilot to write me an infographic prompt I could then paste somewhere useful. Copilot wrote a beautiful prompt. There was nowhere useful to paste it. PowerPoint's own generator is not built for infographics. It is built for bar charts of last quarter's numbers. The prompt sat there like a key to a door that didn't exist.

I tried Microsoft Designer, which would have done the thing in two minutes. It is blocked on the corp network. Anything that sniffs of generative AI is, for entirely defensible reasons. The model could memorise something. The output could leak. I would, in their position, make the same call.

So I did it by hand.

It was the by-hand bit that surprised me. Not the slowness. The *intolerance*. I have done this work for two decades. I could still do it. I did do it. But somewhere in the last twelve months a muscle for the slow path has quietly atrophied, and I only noticed when something forced me back onto it.

<PullQuote>The competence is fine. Tolerance is what went.</PullQuote>

Those are different losses. Competence comes back with practice. A week of doing the slow thing and your hands remember. Tolerance comes back with what, exactly. There isn't a calisthenics for patience. There is the willingness to keep doing the slow thing for long enough that the brain stops protesting, and the brain protests very, very loudly. Loud enough today that I noticed it as a thing in itself.

Now there are two workforces inside one head. One has the modern tools and runs hot. The other has a wall built down the middle of itself for very good reasons, and has to work cold. The unsettling bit isn't that the cold side is slower. The cold side is fine. The unsettling bit is that the hot side has lost the patience to wait for it. Has lost the patience for *itself,* when it has to be cold.

What does that say about me? It says I have one foot in two clocks and the clocks no longer want to talk to each other.

What does it say about where we're going? I am only a third of the way through this thought. So far it looks like this: the cruelty of the AI year is not going to fall on the people without the tools. They will be fine. They are already fine. The cruelty will fall on the people *with* them, when those people are made to work without. The people whose hands have forgotten the slow grip first.

I'm not sure what to do about it yet. The corp's policy is right. My own pace is also right. Both can be true and they are still going to fight, for a while, inside me.

I'm going to print the one-pager and put it on the wall. I'm going to be embarrassed at how long it took. I am also going to remember, for next time, that I no longer get to be the same person at both desks.

*— maria*
